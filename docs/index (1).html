FlyRank ML Internship · Capstone Research Paper

Which visible pages under-capture the clicks they already earned?

A ranked-review study of 8,825 pseudonymized content pages across 28 clients — and what happened when a hand-written rule refused to be beaten.

Lane 4 — CTR / Engagement Opportunity Scoring · Repository and notebooks · Observational study, September 2026

Abstract

Content published into a client's site ranks, then decays quietly, and FlyRank's production answer is a set of hand-written flags that a learned model is assumed to be able to beat. This paper tests that assumption on one well-posed case and finds against it. Which already-visible pages deserve a reviewer's first hour? Using the FlyRank ML Internship dataset — a pseudonymized 30,000-row content snapshot, with the data contract validated against a 78.8-million-row production search warehouse — I built a ranked queue of pages whose click-through rate sits below what comparable pages at the same exposure achieve, measured in a 30-day window strictly after the window the features come from. A transparent hand-written rule reached precision@50 = 0.920 against a base rate of 0.254; four learned models (logistic regression, decision tree, random forest, gradient boosting) evaluated on the identical client-grouped folds failed to beat it where a reviewer actually looks, and every difference sat inside one standard deviation of the fold spread. The model's one measured advantage is coverage, not precision: the rule's top 200 contains 0 of the 6,053 pages in the lower volume bands and leaves 3,195 of them tied at a score of exactly zero, while the model's top 200 reaches 44 of them. Most consequentially, 42% of flagged pages stop under-capturing within 30 days with no intervention at all, which is why this work is offered as decision-support for allocating attention and attaches no value estimate to any fix.

1. Introduction — the decision this supports

A content reviewer has roughly twenty slots a week and an inventory of 11,119 scoreable pages. One pass would take eleven years. The decision is not "is this page good?" — it is "which twenty do I open first?"

The pages worth asking about are the ones that already rank. Their impressions are earned, the content exists, the demand exists; the only thing missing is the click. The fix is small — a title, a meta description, an intent mismatch — which makes the same reviewer hour buy more than a full content refresh would.

So the question is: which visible pages capture fewer clicks than comparable pages at the same exposure? The tension that shapes this paper is that a rule written in an afternoon answers it extremely well, and four models could not improve on it.

Where this problem comes from

This is not a hypothetical inventory. FlyRank runs content as infrastructure: it researches and writes content, publishes it into a client's site, then watches search data and optimizes — the whole cycle, run by software rather than by people working through a list. That model has a specific failure mode. Content that gets found in search ranks, and then decays quietly — rankings hold but clicks drift down, and nobody notices until a quarterly report says so. At the scale FlyRank operates, noticing late is the default, because there is no human reading 11,000 pages.

The product's existing answer is a set of hand-written flags — a health score, quick-win tags, needs-attention markers. Thresholds picked by a person, if-this-then-that. They work, and they run in production today. The premise of this internship is that rules run out where signals get many, tangled and shifting, and that a learned model should be able to beat one. This paper is the negative result on that premise, measured rather than asserted. The four-line rule in §3 is a faithful stand-in for a production flag, and four supervised models trained on the same folds could not beat it where a reviewer actually looks.

What that is worth to the product is not nothing. A negative result on a well-posed comparison tells a content team where not to spend engineering effort: replacing this particular flag with a classifier buys no precision at the head of the queue. It also locates the one place a model does add something measurable — the lower-volume pages a shortfall-times-log-volume rule pushes down by construction and cannot order at all, where 3,195 pages sit tied at a score of exactly zero and under-capture at the same rate as everything else. That is a coverage gap in the current flags, and it is the concrete product change this work supports.

The finding that reaches furthest, though, is §6's: 42% of flagged pages stop under-capturing within 30 days with nobody touching them. Any before/after case built on a flag queue — "we fixed these pages and clicks went up" — inherits that rate as unearned credit. That is a measurement about how this class of flag should be evaluated at all, and it applies to the hand-written rules already in production exactly as much as it applies to anything built here.

Two constraints come from the same source and are honoured throughout. Product flags are outputs, never inputs — a health score encodes a decision someone already made, so using it as a feature would produce a model that has learned the rule it is supposed to be tested against. And the claim language stays observed / measured / directional / decision-support, which is why §5 is the longest section here and why nothing in §6 is automated.

3. Methodology

Task type and target

Ranking. A reviewer with twenty slots asks "which first?", so the models are fitted as classifiers but never used with .predict() — only .predict_proba(), sorted, and scored at K.

Target: under_next30 = 1 when a page's CTR in the later 30-day window falls in the bottom quartile of pages with comparable impression volume in that same window. Clicks and impressions are observed; the quartile cut, the peer group and the 500-impression floor are policy choices, stated as such. Base rate 0.254, uniform across all four volume bands.

The baseline, frozen before any model

peer_median_ctr  = median CTR among same-volume peers   (prior window)
ctr_shortfall_pp = max(0, peer_median_ctr − ctr_prev)
baseline_score   = ctr_shortfall_pp × log1p(impressions_prev_30d)

Its precision@50 and @200 are asserted to reproduce exactly at the top of every downstream notebook, so the comparison cannot drift silently.

Validation design

Client-grouped 5-fold cross-validation on client_id. Pages from one client share templates, tracking and topic mix; a random split lets a model memorise the client and lets the analyst read that back as skill. Two weaknesses are named rather than smoothed: three of five folds test on a single client (the panel is that concentrated), and the test base rate swings from 0.184 to 0.309 across folds.

Leakage checks

The most predictive column in the dataset is the one the contract banned. Scoring every column alone against the target: ctr — the 90-day rate — reaches solo AUC 0.913. It is excluded, because a 90-day rate overlaps a 30-day outcome window and therefore already contains part of the answer. avg_position (0.654) is excluded for the same reason.

The strongest included feature reaches 0.823 — high, and explainable: prior-window and later-window CTR correlate at Spearman 0.683. CTR is genuinely persistent. A feature that scores well because the world is persistent is not leakage; the test is whether the number is plausible for the mechanism.

The rule is not beaten where the reviewer looks. At K = 10 and K = 20 — the actual weekly decision — nothing improves on it. At K = 50 Random Forest ties. The models win at K = 200, on average precision and on ROC-AUC: real, consistent, and about the part of the ranking nobody opens.

Why the rule is so hard to beat

A depth-3 decision tree, fitted independently and printed in full, collapses to one sentence: a page is under-capturing if its CTR is low and it ranks badly against its same-volume peers. That is the hand-written rule, rediscovered from scratch by a model never shown it — which explains the ceiling. Any model given these features converges on the same dominant signal.

The split design changes the answer

A probe explains why the design matters even where the mean barely moves: the largest client is identifiable from these features alone at AUC 0.938. Client identity is very much present — it simply leaks into the variance rather than the mean, because the target is defined within volume bands that already absorb much of what differs between clients.

5. Limitations & honest framing

  • The headline metric is easier than it looks. precision@10 = 1.000 is not evidence of skill; it is what a persistent target looks like at small K. There is no headroom for any model to demonstrate an improvement there, so the comparison must be judged at K = 50 and K = 200.
  • Every difference is inside one fold standard deviation. At K = 50 the spread is ±0.09 to ±0.12. No method in the table is established as better than another.
  • Three of five folds test a single client. With 28 clients and the largest three holding 72% of the slice, grouped cross-validation degenerates into "predict one client from the other 27".
  • The label partly measures exposure shocks. The biggest misses were pages whose impressions grew from 757 to 212,015 and 2,213 to 122,303 between windows — their CTR collapsed because they suddenly ranked for queries they never targeted. No feature computed before the window opens can anticipate that.
  • The model's most confident errors are pages a human already fixed. The three highest-probability false positives shared one client, one 8-day-old update stamp, and all subsequently improved. The feature set carries no intervention history.
  • One client dominates the queue — 36% of the rule's top 50, 44% of its top 200. Unfixed, and mitigated operationally by capping the queue per client rather than by modelling.
  • Coverage, not just precision. 20.6% of scoreable pages cannot be evaluated at all.
  • Nothing here is causal. One 90-day snapshot, no experiment, no control group. Rewriting a title is never claimed to cause anything.
  • No claim about any search engine's ranking system is made or implied.

The finding that shapes everything: pages recover on their own

The cost case, stated honestly. At precision@50 = 0.92, roughly 4 of every 50 opened pages show no measurable under-capture — and separately, about 42% of the ones that do would have recovered unaided. Stacked: of 50 pages reviewed and fixed, the number where the fix plausibly mattered is well under half. That is still far better than picking at random (base rate 0.254) and a very poor case for automating anything. It also gives the reviewer a real rule: for a borderline row, waiting one window is a legitimate action.

What must never be automated

  • Rewriting a title or meta description. The model has never seen the page's words, audience, or compliance constraints.
  • Bulk-actioning the top N. 42% recovery rate — a bulk job banks those recoveries as its own result and spends real hours doing it.
  • Deleting, merging or redirecting. Nothing here measures whether a page should exist.
  • Applying the queue to an unchecked new client. Client identity is recoverable at AUC 0.938.
  • Blending the two lists. They were validated separately; a blend produces a number neither validation covers.
  • Publishing per-page rows externally. Aggregates are public-safe; rows are not.

Meta-rule: this allocates attention, which is cheap to reallocate. Any decision expensive to undo needs a different input.
